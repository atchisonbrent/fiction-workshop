# Small Mercy

The customs desk on Lacuna Station had three temperatures.

Green meant the request could be approved. Amber meant it could be approved by somebody who had an office. Red meant the station would produce a form explaining that physics had regrettably failed to budget for the applicant.

The request on Nera Vale’s screen was red.

“Heat transfer,” she read. “External vessel. Twelve minutes.”

The ship outside had no declared name, only a registry scar where one had been burned off. It rolled slowly beyond the customs glass, a dark spindle against the planet’s white limb. Frost filmed its radiator vanes. Its transponder claimed agricultural machinery. Its life-sign return claimed eighty-three people.

Those two facts regarded one another with official suspicion.

Nera opened the channel. “Unregistered vessel, Lacuna Customs. State the purpose of transfer.”

Static answered first. Then a woman said, “Restarting the drive.”

“The request category says environmental support.”

“Your system rejected propulsion assistance.”

“It will reject environmental support too.”

“Yes.”

Nera glanced at the station allocation. The reactor had lost another point overnight. Twelve minutes sent outside meant twelve minutes taken from somewhere inside: machine galleries, storage, the old habitation ring where families slept in coats and called it normal.

Beyond the glass, the spindle continued its patient roll.

“Why is your drive cold?” Nera asked.

A pause. “Because we shut it down to cross the patrol line.”

There it was. The sentence that relieved everybody of complexity.

Nera selected **DENY**. The system opened a list of reasons, each written in the calm dialect of a person who would never hear the answer.

The woman said, “Customs Officer?”

“You entered Directorate space without clearance.”

“We entered it without being fired upon. Clearance would have spoiled that.”

Nera almost smiled. The woman sounded exhausted rather than brave, which was generally where bravery lived when it had actual work to do.

A patrol marker appeared at the edge of the plot. Twenty-seven minutes away.

“How long since shutdown?” Nera asked.

“Six hours.”

“Then twelve minutes won’t restart a fusion plant.”

“No. It will thaw the catalyst valves.”

“And after that?”

“We have our own fuel.”

The station chimed. **ALLOCATION VARIANCE DETECTED.** Somewhere behind Nera, an automatic shutter closed to preserve heat in an empty conference room. Lacuna was very good at surviving its own priorities.

She pulled up the ring schedule. The old habitation ring was due eighteen minutes of warmth at the top of the hour. Eight hundred and six people. Delay it by twelve minutes and nobody would die. That was not the same as saying nobody would pay.

“How many children?” she asked.

The woman did not answer immediately. Nera respected her for that.

“Thirty-one.”

“Any in medical cold-sleep?”

“Four. The youngest is two.”

Facts, once supplied, were vulgar things. They stood in the room and made furniture of every principle that had seemed important before their arrival.

Nera looked at the patrol marker. Twenty-four minutes.

The customs system had prepared its denial:

> REQUEST EXCEEDS LAWFUL EXTERNAL THERMAL ALLOCATION. APPLICANT MAY SEEK REVIEW WITHIN TEN STANDARD DAYS.

Efficient. Fair. Entirely reusable after the applicants were dead.

She routed the response to her private queue and opened maintenance control. Customs officers did not have authority over station heat. They did, however, have authority to quarantine contaminated cargo, and contaminated cargo was entitled to thermal isolation.

Nera designated Dock Sleeve Four as a biological hazard.

The station asked for a contamination code.

She entered the first thing that came to mind: **MERCY**.

The station rejected it for containing letters.

Nera entered **63729**.

Dock Sleeve Four warmed. The old ring’s allocation slipped twelve minutes down the schedule. In apartments she would never visit, eight hundred and six people remained cold without knowing why.

Outside, vapor burst from the spindle’s vanes.

The woman on the channel inhaled sharply. “Transfer received.”

“You have twelve minutes.”

“What do you want?”

Nera watched the patrol marker cross into twenty minutes.

The sensible answer was coordinates, testimony, names—some future payment with which to purchase a cleaner interpretation of the present. She found she wanted none of them.

“Leave before I become evidence,” she said.

The spindle stopped rolling. Heat moved through it in dim orange lines, one compartment after another. For a moment the ship looked less like machinery than a city seen from orbit: fragile, improbable, insisting on itself.

At eleven minutes, its drive lit.

At eleven minutes and forty seconds, it moved.

At twelve, Nera ended the transfer and restored the old ring’s place in the queue. The spindle dwindled toward the planet’s shadow. The patrol altered course, too late and with the offended precision of a door closing after the guest had climbed out the window.

The customs system displayed three unresolved violations. The contamination code would survive perhaps an hour of inspection. The heat ledger, somewhat less.

“Customs Officer Vale,” said the station supervisor over the internal channel. “Why is Sleeve Four reporting a quarantine?”

Nera opened the prepared denial. She changed **REQUEST** to **REQUESTED VESSEL** and sent it into the archive. It was a small forgery, grammatically perfect.

Then she put both hands on the desk and waited for the room to become colder.
